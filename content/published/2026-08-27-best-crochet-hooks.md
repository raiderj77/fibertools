# Best Crochet Hooks: How to Pick One That Actually Works for You

The best crochet hook is the one that matches your yarn weight, fits your hand without strain, and has a shaft finish your yarn glides on. For most crocheters, that means an ergonomic or comfort-grip hook in aluminum or wood, sized to the yarn label. There is no single "best" brand. There is a best hook for your project and your hands.

## Why does my hand cramp up when I crochet?

Your hand cramps because the hook is too thin, too heavy, or shaped wrong for how you grip it. A hook that digs into your palm or fingers will cause pain no matter how good your stitches look. The fix is almost always a grip change, not a willpower problem.

Most hand pain comes from one of three things.

First, a hook that is too skinny. Basic aluminum hooks have a thin, straight shaft. If you hold your yarn tight and grip hard, that thin metal presses into your fingers for hours. Over a full sweater, that adds up.

Second, a handle with no grip surface. Slick metal or bare wood can make you squeeze harder just to keep control. Squeezing harder tires your hand faster.

Third, wrong hook weight for the job. A heavy hook feels fine for a few rows. It feels different after a full evening of crochet.

The fix is to try an ergonomic hook. These have a molded handle, usually rubber or soft plastic, shaped to rest in your palm. Brands like Clover Amour, Furls, and Susan Bates Silvalume all make versions of this. You do not need to buy a full set right away. Buy one hook in a size you use often, like a 5.0mm (H) or 5.5mm (I), and test it for a week.

If you hold your hook like a pencil, look for a hook with a flatter grip area near the thumb rest. If you hold it like a knife, in your fist, look for a rounder, thicker handle. Both grip styles are correct. The hook just needs to match your grip.

## What hook material should I actually buy?

Aluminum hooks work for most yarns and most budgets. Wood or bamboo hooks are better for slippery yarns like silk or bamboo blends, because the surface has more grip. Steel hooks are for thread crochet, not yarn.

Here is how the materials actually perform, not just how they are marketed.

**Aluminum** is the standard. It is smooth, light, and inexpensive. Susan Bates and Boye both make classic aluminum hooks that have been around for decades. Stitches slide easily, which is good for speed but can be bad if your yarn is already slippery, like acrylic or rayon blends. The stitches can slip right off the hook if you are not watching closely.

**Wood and bamboo** hooks have more texture. The yarn catches slightly as it moves, which slows things down just enough to give you more control. This matters a lot with slippery fibers like silk, bamboo, or high-sheen acrylic. Wood hooks also warm up in your hand, which some crocheters find more comfortable for long sessions. The tradeoff is that wood hooks can catch on rough or splitty yarns, like some wools, and slow you down too much.

**Plastic** hooks are common in beginner kits. They are fine for learning, but the tips can be less precise, and larger sizes can flex under pressure. They are the cheapest option and a fine starting point, not usually a long-term favorite.

**Steel** hooks are a different category. They are small, thin, and made for thread crochet, doilies, and fine lace work. If you are working with worsted weight yarn, you do not need a steel hook. If you are working with size 10 crochet thread, you need one.

A practical approach: buy an aluminum or ergonomic hook for your everyday worsted and DK weight projects. Add one or two wood hooks if you work with silk, alpaca blends, or other slippery fibers. You do not need every material. You need the right one for the yarn in front of you.

## How do I know what hook size to use?

Check the yarn label first. It will list a suggested hook size range, usually in millimeters and a US letter or number. Use that as your starting point, then adjust based on your own tension.

Yarn labels are not decoration. That little diagram with the hook and needle icons tells you the yarn weight category and a suggested hook range. A worsted weight (category 4) yarn usually suggests a 5.0mm to 5.5mm hook, or H to I. A DK weight (category 3) usually suggests 4.0mm to 4.5mm, or G to 7.

But labels are a starting point, not a rule. Everyone crochets at a different tension. Some people crochet tight, some loose. This is why gauge swatches matter more than the label alone.

Here is the actual process:

1. Pick a hook size from the yarn label's suggested range.
2. Crochet a swatch at least 4 inches by 4 inches, in the stitch pattern you plan to use.
3. Measure how many stitches and rows fit in 4 inches.
4. Compare that to the gauge listed in your pattern.
5. If you have too many stitches per inch, your fabric is tighter than the pattern wants. Go up a hook size.
6. If you have too few stitches per inch, your fabric is looser than the pattern wants. Go down a hook size.

Always check your gauge before starting a garment, blanket, or anything where size matters. Skipping this step is the most common reason a finished project does not turn out the size you expected.

For simple items like dishcloths or scrap yarn projects, gauge matters less. For sweaters, fitted hats, or anything with a size requirement, it matters a lot.

## Do I need a whole set of hooks, or just a few?

You need fewer hooks than you think. Most crocheters use three or four sizes for 90 percent of their projects. Build your collection slowly, based on the yarn weights you actually use.

Hook sets look appealing because they seem like a complete solution. But a 12-piece set often includes sizes you will rarely touch, like 2.5mm steel hooks if you never do thread work, or a 10mm hook if you never work with bulky yarn.

Instead, think about what you actually make. If you mostly crochet baby blankets and dishcloths in worsted weight cotton or acrylic, you mainly need hooks in the 4.5mm to 5.5mm range (G through I). If you make garments in DK or fingering weight, you will use smaller hooks, closer to 3.5mm to 4.5mm (E through 7).

A practical starter set:

- 4.0mm (G) for DK weight and lighter projects
- 5.0mm (H) for worsted weight, the most common yarn weight
- 5.5mm (I) for worsted weight crocheted looser, or lightly textured stitches
- 6.5mm (K) for bulky yarn projects like chunky scarves

From there, add sizes as your projects demand them. If you start working with fingering weight yarn for shawls, add a 3.5mm (E) or 3.75mm (F). If you get into thread crochet, that is a separate small investment in steel hooks, sized much smaller, often 1.0mm to 1.75mm.

Buying one good hook at a time, based on a real project need, will serve you better than buying a full set upfront.

## Are expensive hooks actually worth the money?

Sometimes, yes. A well-made ergonomic hook can prevent hand pain over years of use, which is worth paying for. But an expensive hook will not make you a better crocheter, and a cheap hook will not hold you back from finishing a beautiful project.

Higher-priced hooks, like Furls wood hooks or Lykke sets, often have better finished surfaces, more comfortable handles, and nicer packaging. Some crocheters find the added weight and balance genuinely improves their speed and comfort over long projects.

But plenty of experienced crocheters still use basic Susan Bates or Boye hooks that cost a few dollars each. The stitches look the same either way. What you're really paying for with premium hooks is comfort, durability, and sometimes a nicer hook tip shape for specific techniques like Tunisian crochet or amigurumi.

If your hands hurt using budget hooks, it is worth trying one ergonomic hook before assuming you need pain relief tools or need to crochet less. If your hands feel fine, there is no reason to upgrade just because a hook looks nicer.

## What is the difference between an inline hook and a tapered hook?

An inline hook has a hook head that lines up directly with the shaft, like a flat blade. A tapered hook has a more rounded, sloped head that narrows gradually. This changes how the yarn catches on the hook, and it is a matter of personal preference, not one being objectively better.

Boye hooks are the classic example of tapered hooks. Susan Bates hooks are the classic example of inline hooks. Some crocheters swear by one style and find the other awkward. Others do not notice a difference at all.

If you have never compared them, it is worth trying both styles in the same size. Crochet a few rows of single crochet with each. Notice if one feels like it catches the yarn more smoothly, or if one gives you more consistent stitch size. There is no wrong answer here. Your hands will tell you which one they prefer.

## Bringing it together

The best crochet hook fits your hand, matches your yarn, and does not fight you while you work. Start with the yarn label's suggested size, always swatch and measure as you go, and pay attention to hand comfort over multiple sessions, not just the first ten minutes. Build your hook collection slowly, based on the projects you actually make, not the size of the set on sale. A good hook should disappear in your hand and let you focus on the stitches, not on the tool itself.

## Frequently asked questions

### What is the best crochet hook for beginners?
Ergonomic hooks with cushioned grips, like those from Clover Amour or Furls Streamline, are best for beginners. These hooks reduce hand strain while you're still learning tension control and hook rotation. Look for a size range that includes common starter sizes (H-8 or 5mm) and clear, easy-to-read size markings on the shaft. Many beginner sets also include multiple sizes in one kit, letting you experiment with different projects without buying hooks individually as your skills develop.

### What size crochet hook should I use?
The right hook size depends on your yarn weight and desired fabric density, and is usually listed on the yarn label. Worsted weight yarn typically pairs with a 5–6mm (H-J) hook, while lace-weight yarn needs a much smaller 1.5–2.5mm hook. If you want tighter, sturdier stitches (like for amigurumi), size down; for drapier fabric, size up. Always check your gauge swatch against the pattern's recommended gauge before starting a full project.

### Are ergonomic crochet hooks worth it?
Yes, ergonomic crochet hooks are worth it if you crochet frequently or experience hand or wrist fatigue. Their wider, contoured handles distribute pressure more evenly across your grip compared to traditional straight hooks, which can reduce strain during long sessions. They're especially beneficial for people with arthritis or repetitive strain concerns. While they cost more than basic aluminum hooks, many crafters find the comfort improvement makes them a worthwhile investment for regular use.

### What's the difference between aluminum and wood crochet hooks?
Aluminum hooks are smooth, fast, and durable, making them ideal for slippery yarns and quick stitch work, while wood hooks offer more grip and warmth in hand. Wood hooks tend to be lighter and quieter, and many crafters prefer them for tactile comfort during long projects. Aluminum hooks generally last longer and resist breaking, while wood hooks can feel more comfortable in colder climates since metal retains less body heat.

### Do I need a full set of crochet hooks or just a few sizes?
Most crocheters only need a handful of core sizes, not a full set, unless they work with many different yarn weights. Starting with 3.5mm, 4mm, 5mm, and 6mm covers the most common patterns for worsted and DK weight yarn. A full set becomes useful once you branch into lace work, bulky yarn, or specialty projects like amigurumi. Buying individual hooks as needed is often more cost-effective than purchasing a complete boxed set upfront.
