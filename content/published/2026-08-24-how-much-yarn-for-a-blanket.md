# How Much Yarn for a Blanket?

Most throw blankets need 1,800 to 3,000 yards of worsted weight yarn. Twin size blankets often need 3,500 to 5,000 yards. The exact amount depends on your yarn weight, stitch pattern, and blanket size. Buy a little extra. Running out mid-project is the real problem here.

## Do You Just Multiply Yardage by Blanket Size?

No, this only gets you a rough starting point. Blanket size alone does not tell you how much yarn you need. Stitch pattern and gauge matter just as much.

A lot of knitters and crocheters pull up a chart, see "3,000 yards for a throw," and stop there. That number assumes a specific yarn weight and a plain stitch like stockinette or single crochet. Change the stitch and the number changes too.

Cables eat yarn. A cabled blanket can use 20% more yarn than the same size blanket in plain stockinette. The yarn has to travel over and under itself, so it uses more length per stitch.

Lace does the opposite. Lace patterns have open holes, so they often use less yarn than a solid fabric of the same size.

Crochet has its own version of this problem. A double crochet blanket uses less yarn than the same size blanket in single crochet, because single crochet is a denser stitch. But single crochet fabric is often warmer and heavier for the same size.

**How to avoid it:** Find yardage estimates that match your actual stitch pattern, not just your blanket size. If you can't find an exact match, make a swatch first. Weigh it, measure it, then do the math for your full blanket size.

## Why Did I Run Out of Yarn Two Rows Before the End?

This happens because knitters and crocheters usually buy for the "average" project and don't account for their own gauge, tension, or pattern changes. Your gauge might be tighter or looser than the pattern writer's gauge. That alone can shift your yarn needs by hundreds of yards.

Gauge is the number of stitches and rows per inch. Two people using the same yarn and the same needle size can still get different gauges. One person knits tight, another knits loose. If your gauge is tighter than the pattern's gauge, you need more yarn to reach the same size. Tighter stitches pack more yarn into the same space.

Pattern changes matter too. Maybe you added an extra repeat of a cable panel. Maybe you swapped single crochet for half double crochet in one section. Small changes add up over a large project like a blanket.

Border and edging also get forgotten. A lot of blanket patterns add a border after the main body is done. That border can use 200 to 500 extra yards, depending on width and stitch. If you don't plan for it, you might finish the body and have nothing left for the edge.

**How to avoid it:** Always check your gauge before you start. Knit or crochet a swatch, block it the way you'll block the final blanket, then measure. If your gauge is off from the pattern, adjust your yarn estimate up or down. Buy extra for borders and edging, even if the pattern includes yardage for them. Patterns are written by someone with their own gauge, not yours.

## Is It Safe to Buy the Exact Yardage the Pattern Calls For?

Not really. Dye lots vary, tension varies, and mistakes happen. Buying the exact amount leaves no room for any of that.

Dye lots are one of the biggest hidden risks. Yarn is dyed in batches. Even the same color number can look slightly different between batches. If you buy the exact yardage and need one more skein later, you might not be able to match the color. This is especially true for hand-dyed yarn, where every skein can be a little different even within the same dye lot.

Frogging (ripping out stitches) also eats into your yarn supply. Every knitter and crocheter makes mistakes. You rip out a section, some yarn gets used up in the process, and now you have less than you planned for.

Swatching uses yarn too. If you swatch with yarn from your project skeins instead of a separate scrap skein, you've already spent yardage before you even cast on.

**How to avoid it:** Buy at least one extra skein beyond what the pattern calls for. For a large project like a blanket, some fiber crafters buy two extra skeins, especially with hand-dyed or hard-to-match yarn. Check that all your skeins share the same dye lot number before you leave the store or check out online. Keep receipts or order info until the blanket is done, in case you need to order more from the same shop.

## Does Yarn Weight Change How Much I Need?

Yes, a lot. Thicker yarn covers more area per yard, so you need fewer yards. Thinner yarn needs more yards to cover the same size blanket.

Think about it this way. A bulky weight yarn might get you 2 to 3 stitches per inch. A fingering weight yarn might get you 7 to 8 stitches per inch. To cover the same size blanket, the fingering weight project needs far more stitches, and each stitch uses yarn. That means a lot more total yardage.

Here's a rough comparison for a 50 inch by 60 inch throw blanket:

- Bulky weight: around 1,200 to 1,800 yards
- Worsted weight: around 1,800 to 2,800 yards
- DK weight: around 2,800 to 3,800 yards
- Fingering weight: around 4,000 to 6,000 yards

These are rough ranges. Your stitch pattern and gauge will move these numbers up or down. But the trend is clear. Lighter yarn weight means more yardage needed for the same size blanket.

**How to avoid it:** Don't reuse a yardage estimate from a different yarn weight project. If you're switching a pattern from worsted to DK weight, redo your yardage math. Use the yarn weight's typical yards per skein (check the label) and estimate how many skeins you'll need based on total yardage required, not skein count from a different weight.

## Should I Trust the Yardage Listed on a Pattern I Found Online?

Trust it as a starting point, not a guarantee. Free patterns especially can have errors, outdated yardage estimates, or numbers that assume a specific brand of yarn you might not be using.

Patterns are written by real people, and people make mistakes. A pattern might list yardage based on the exact yarn brand the designer used. If you substitute a different yarn, even in the same weight category, the yardage might not match exactly. Yarn thickness can vary slightly between brands, even within the same weight class like worsted or DK.

Older patterns can also be a problem. Yarn put up (the amount per skein) has changed over the decades. A pattern from the 1990s might assume skeins that held more or less yardage than today's typical skein. If the pattern lists "8 skeins" instead of a yardage number, that instruction might not translate well to modern yarn.

Some free patterns also just have typos or math errors in the materials section. It happens more than people think.

**How to avoid it:** Look for a total yardage number, not just a skein count, whenever possible. If a pattern only gives skein counts, check what yardage those skeins were based on and do the math yourself for your chosen yarn. Read pattern reviews or comments if you're using a site like Ravelry. Other crafters who made the same pattern often mention if they ran short or had lots left over.

## Do I Need to Plan Differently for a Striped or Multi-Color Blanket?

Yes. Color changes mean you need to estimate yardage separately for each color, not just for the blanket as a whole. Running out of one color mid-stripe is a common and frustrating problem.

With a solid color blanket, you just need a total yardage number. With stripes or color work, you need to know how much of each color you'll use. A wide stripe uses more yarn than a thin stripe. A blanket with five colors in equal stripes needs roughly one-fifth of the total yardage in each color, but only if the stripes are truly equal in width and stitch count.

Colorwork patterns like fair isle or intarsia add more complexity. Some colors get used a lot (background colors), and some get used a little (accent colors). A small accent color might only need one skein for the whole blanket, while the background color might need six or seven.

Granny square blankets and other crochet motif blankets have their own version of this. If you're making 30 squares in four rotating colors, you need enough of each color to finish all the squares that use it, plus enough for joining if you're using a contrast join color.

**How to avoid it:** Break your yardage estimate down by color, not just by total blanket size. For striped projects, calculate yardage per stripe width, then multiply by how many stripes will use that color. For motif blankets, count how many squares or motifs need each color before you buy. Buy a little extra of every color, since color needs are harder to estimate exactly than total yardage.

## What's the Safest Way to Estimate Yarn for a Big Blanket?

The safest method combines a yardage chart, your own gauge swatch, and a buffer of extra yarn. No single method alone is reliable enough for a project this size.

Start with a general yardage chart based on your blanket size and yarn weight. This gives you a ballpark. Then knit or crochet a swatch in your actual stitch pattern, weigh it, and calculate yards used per square inch. Multiply that by your total blanket area for a more personalized number.

Finally, add a buffer. For a project this size, 10% extra yardage is a reasonable cushion. That covers gauge differences, mistakes, borders, and finishing details like seaming or edging that are easy to forget when you first count up materials.

Blankets are a big investment of time and yarn. A little extra planning up front saves you from a stressful yarn shortage halfway through row 200.

## Frequently asked questions

### How much yarn do I need for a queen size blanket?
A queen size blanket typically requires 3,000 to 4,500 yards of worsted weight yarn, or roughly 10 to 15 skeins depending on brand and yardage per skein. The exact amount depends on your stitch pattern, tension, and hook or needle size, since denser stitches like crochet's single crochet use more yarn than open lace patterns. Chunkier yarns need fewer yards but more weight in ounces. Always buy an extra skein or two from the same dye lot to avoid running short before binding off.

### How many skeins of yarn does it take to make a baby blanket?
Most baby blankets need 4 to 6 skeins of worsted weight yarn, covering about 1,200 to 1,800 yards total for a standard 30x40 inch size. Smaller receiving blankets or lovies may only require 2 to 3 skeins. The finished gauge matters a lot here, as tighter stitches or textured patterns like popcorn stitch consume more yarn than a simple garter or single crochet fabric. Using a yarn calculator with your specific gauge swatch gives the most accurate estimate before you commit to a project.

### How do I calculate yardage for a blanket based on size?
Start by calculating the total square footage of your blanket, then multiply by the yardage-per-square-foot rate for your yarn weight and stitch pattern, typically 130 to 200 yards per square foot for worsted weight. For example, a 50x60 inch throw equals about 20.8 square feet, so you'd need roughly 2,700 to 4,150 yards. Knitting a swatch first and measuring exact yardage used per square inch gives you a personalized rate that accounts for your unique tension and stitch choice.

### Does crochet or knitting use more yarn for a blanket?
Crochet generally uses 20 to 30 percent more yarn than knitting for the same size blanket, because crochet stitches create denser, thicker fabric with more yarn wrapped per stitch. A knit stockinette blanket might use 2,500 yards where an equivalent crochet single crochet blanket needs 3,200 yards or more. Open crochet stitches like granny squares or shells narrow that gap somewhat. If you're deciding between techniques and have limited yarn, knitting or an airy crochet stitch pattern will stretch your stash further.

### What size blanket does one skein of yarn make?
One standard skein of worsted weight yarn, around 200 yards, typically makes only a small square, swatch, or granny square rather than a full blanket. To gauge finished size, knit or crochet a 4x4 inch test swatch, weigh or measure the yarn used, then scale up proportionally to your target blanket dimensions. As a rough benchmark, expect one skein to cover about 1.5 to 2 square feet of worsted weight fabric, so a lap blanket alone needs 8 to 10 skeins.
