# Cross Stitch Fabric Count: What It Means and How to Pick the Right One

Fabric count is the number of stitches per inch on your cross stitch fabric. A 14-count fabric has 14 stitches per inch. A 28-count has 28 half stitches per inch, since most fabric at that count is stitched over two threads. Higher count means smaller stitches and a more detailed look.

## What Does "Count" Actually Mean on Cross Stitch Fabric?

Count tells you how many threads fit in one inch of fabric. More threads packed into that inch means smaller squares to stitch on. This one number controls almost everything about how your finished piece will look.

Fabric makers weave count fabric with evenly spaced threads. When you count 14 threads across one inch, you have 14-count fabric. Each little square formed by the threads holds one cross stitch. So a higher count fabric has smaller squares, and a lower count fabric has bigger squares.

This matters because count decides your stitch size, not your pattern. The pattern chart stays the same no matter what fabric you pick. It's a grid of squares representing stitches. But the size of the finished piece changes based on the count you stitch it on.

Here's a simple way to picture it. A pattern that is 100 stitches wide will measure about 7 inches wide on 14-count fabric. That same pattern will measure about 10 inches wide on 10-count fabric. Same pattern, different fabric, different size.

## Why Does My Cross Stitch Look Different From the Picture?

If your stitches look bigger or smaller than the photo on the pattern, your fabric count does not match the designer's count. This is normal and easy to fix. Just check the count listed on the pattern and compare it to your fabric.

Most cross stitch patterns list a suggested fabric count. This tells you what count the designer used to get the size shown in their photo. If you stitch the same pattern on a different count, your piece will come out a different size.

Say a pattern was designed on 14-count Aida and shows a 5 by 7 inch finished piece. If you stitch it on 18-count fabric instead, the piece will come out smaller, closer to 4 by 5 inches. Stitch it on 11-count fabric, and it grows to around 6.5 by 9 inches.

None of these results are wrong. They are just different sizes. This is actually one of the best things about cross stitch. You can shrink or grow almost any pattern just by switching your fabric count. No math skills required, though a little math helps you plan ahead.

To find your finished size before you start, divide the stitch count by the fabric count. If your pattern is 140 stitches wide and you're using 14-count fabric, that's 140 divided by 14, which equals 10 inches. Switch to 20-count and it's 140 divided by 20, which equals 7 inches.

## What's the Difference Between Aida and Evenweave Count?

Aida fabric has count numbers like 11, 14, 16, and 18. Evenweave and linen use higher numbers like 25, 28, and 32, but you usually stitch over two threads at a time. This makes the actual stitch size closer to half the printed count number.

Aida is the fabric most beginners start with. It has a clear grid woven right into it, with holes at each corner of every square. This makes it easy to see exactly where your needle goes. One square equals one cross stitch. Simple and predictable.

Evenweave and linen work differently. These fabrics have threads woven in a tight, even pattern, but no obvious blocks or holes marking each stitch. Instead, stitchers usually work "over two," meaning each cross stitch covers two threads in each direction instead of one.

This changes the math. A 28-count evenweave stitched over two threads gives you roughly 14 stitches per inch in practice, even though the fabric itself has 28 threads per inch. That's why 28-count evenweave and 14-count Aida often produce very similar-looking results, even though the count numbers look completely different.

Linen almost always gets stitched over two threads because the weave is naturally a little bit uneven. Stitching over two threads averages out small gaps and gives you a steadier, more even stitch.

If a pattern calls for 32-count linen and doesn't say otherwise, assume it means stitched over two threads. Check the pattern instructions to be sure, since some designers do specify stitching over one thread on lower counts for extra detail work.

## How Do I Choose the Right Count for a Project?

Pick a lower count like 11 or 14 for large, simple designs and easier stitching. Pick a higher count like 18, 28, or 32 for small, detailed designs like portraits or fine text. Your eyesight and patience matter just as much as the pattern itself.

Lower count fabric, like 11-count or 14-count Aida, has bigger squares. Bigger squares mean bigger stitches. This makes the fabric easier to see and easier to thread through, especially for new stitchers or anyone working without magnification. Kids' projects, holiday ornaments, and bold graphic designs all do well on lower count fabric.

Higher count fabric, like 18-count, 25-count, or 32-count, packs in far more detail per inch. This is what makes portrait-style cross stitch and finely shaded designs possible. But smaller squares mean smaller stitches, which means more strain on your eyes and more patience at the needle.

Think about where you'll be stitching, too. If you stitch mostly at night under a lamp, or if you don't want to reach for reading glasses every five minutes, a lower count will be much friendlier. If you love fine detail and don't mind slower progress, higher count opens up a lot more design options.

There's also a practical trick worth knowing. You can stitch a design meant for 14-count fabric on 28-count evenweave over two threads. This gives you the same finished size as the original 14-count version, but on a nicer, often softer fabric. Some stitchers prefer the drape and texture of evenweave over Aida, even for simple designs.

## Does Fabric Count Affect How Much Floss I Need?

Yes, but not in a huge way. Higher count fabric uses slightly less floss per square inch because the stitches are smaller. Lower count fabric uses a bit more floss because each stitch covers more space. The difference is rarely something you need to plan around closely.

Floss usage depends mostly on the total number of stitches in your design, not the fabric count. A design with 10,000 stitches uses roughly the same total length of floss whether you stitch it on 11-count or 18-count fabric. The size of each stitch changes, but the number of stitches stays the same.

Where count does matter a little is in strand count. Higher count fabric, especially 22-count and up, usually calls for stitching with fewer strands of floss, often just one strand instead of two. This keeps the stitches from looking too bulky and covering the tiny fabric squares completely. Lower count fabric almost always uses two strands for regular cross stitch, since the bigger squares need more coverage.

Check your pattern's key for strand recommendations. If it doesn't list any, a good general rule is two strands for 11 to 16-count fabric, and one strand for 18-count and higher when stitching over one thread.

## How Do I Know What Count My Fabric Already Is?

Check the fabric label or the edge of the fabric bolt, since count is almost always printed there. If you've lost the label, count the threads yourself across one inch using a ruler and a magnifier if needed.

To count by hand, lay your ruler flat against the fabric. Look at one inch of space and count the individual threads running across it, not the woven squares. This takes a steady hand and good light, but it works in a pinch.

A specialty tool called a thread counter, sometimes sold as a linen tester, makes this much easier. It's a small magnifying loupe with a marked window that sits right on the fabric. You look through it, count the threads inside the window, and get an accurate count in seconds. Many fiber shops carry these near the cross stitch supplies.

If you're stitching on fabric from a kit and the packaging is long gone, this method is worth the few minutes it takes. Guessing at count can throw off your whole project size, especially on bigger designs.

## What's a Good Starting Count for a Beginner?

14-count Aida is the most common starting point for new cross stitchers. It's affordable, widely available, and easy to see clearly without strain. Most beginner patterns are designed with 14-count in mind, so charts and kits will match up smoothly.

Once you've stitched a few projects on 14-count and feel comfortable with the basic cross stitch, try moving up to 16-count or over to evenweave. This gives you a feel for how count changes the stitching experience without jumping straight into very fine detail work.

There's no rule that says you have to move up in count over time. Plenty of experienced stitchers stick with 14-count for most of their projects because they like the size and the pace. Count is a creative choice, not a skill ladder you're required to climb.

Whatever count you choose, measure your fabric before you cut it and leave a few extra inches of margin around your design for finishing and framing later. Check your gauge on a small test swatch first if you're trying a brand new fabric type, just to see how your tension looks before committing to the full piece.

## Frequently asked questions

### What does fabric count mean in cross stitch?
Fabric count refers to the number of threads or stitches per inch on your cross stitch fabric, determining how large or small your finished design will be. A higher count, like 18 or 22, means smaller stitches and finer detail, while a lower count, like 11 or 14, produces larger, bolder stitches. Aida cloth is measured this way, and evenweave fabrics are counted by threads per inch instead. Choosing the right count affects both the finished size of your project and how easy it is to see your stitches while working.

### What is the most common fabric count for beginners?
14-count Aida is the most common choice for beginners because it offers a good balance between visible stitches and manageable detail. Each stitch is large enough to count easily and work with standard embroidery floss without excessive eye strain. It also has clearly defined holes, making needle placement simpler for new stitchers. Many beginner kits and patterns are designed specifically for 14-count fabric, so starting here helps new crafters build confidence before moving to finer counts like 16 or 18 for more intricate designs.

### How do I calculate finished design size based on fabric count?
To calculate finished size, divide the stitch count of your pattern by the fabric count, then add a few inches for margins. For example, a 140-stitch-wide design on 14-count Aida equals 10 inches (140 ÷ 14), before adding border space. This calculation works for both width and height separately. Higher fabric counts shrink the finished size, while lower counts enlarge it. Always check whether your pattern specifies stitches-per-inch or thread count, since evenweave fabrics may require stitching over two threads, effectively halving the count.

### What's the difference between Aida and evenweave fabric count?
Aida fabric count refers to stitches per inch, with each block representing one cross stitch, while evenweave fabric count refers to threads per inch, typically stitched over two threads. This means 28-count evenweave produces stitches similar in size to 14-count Aida, since you're working over two threads at once. Evenweave offers a smoother, more linen-like finish popular for heirloom projects, while Aida's grid structure makes it easier for beginners to count stitches accurately without confusion.

### Can I change the fabric count listed in a pattern?
Yes, you can substitute a different fabric count than what's listed, but it will change your finished project's dimensions. Switching from 14-count to 18-count, for instance, makes the design smaller and more compact since more stitches fit per inch. Always recalculate the finished size using the pattern's stitch count and your chosen fabric count before purchasing materials. This flexibility lets you customize projects for framing needs, personal preference, or matching fabric you already own in your stash.
