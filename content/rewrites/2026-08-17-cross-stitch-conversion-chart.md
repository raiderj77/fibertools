# Cross Stitch Conversion Chart: How to Turn Any Pattern Into Crochet or Knit

A cross stitch conversion chart lets you turn any cross stitch pattern into a grid you can crochet or knit. Each stitch on the cross stitch chart becomes one square, block, or stitch in your craft. You just need to match the grid size and pick your method: single crochet, tapestry crochet, or knit colorwork.

## What Is a Cross Stitch Conversion Chart?

A cross stitch conversion chart is a grid pattern made for embroidery that you translate into stitches for crochet or knitting. Each colored square on the chart becomes one stitch in your project. The count and layout stay the same. Only the tool and the stitch change.

Cross stitch patterns are already built on a grid. Each square usually stands for one X stitch made with thread on fabric. That grid is really just a pixel map. Crochet and knitting use grids too. A single crochet worked in the round or in rows can hold one color per stitch. A knit stitch can do the same thing with stranded colorwork or intarsia. So the chart doesn't change. Only what you do with each square changes.

This matters because cross stitch has thousands of free and paid patterns. Corner-to-corner crochet (C2C), single crochet graphghans, and knit colorwork charts all use the same idea. If you can read a cross stitch chart, you already know how to read most crochet and knit charts too.

## Why Convert a Cross Stitch Chart Instead of Using a Crochet Chart?

Cross stitch charts are convert-friendly because they are simple and widely available. Crochet-specific graph patterns can be harder to find for certain images. Cross stitch gives you more design choices with less searching.

Search any craft site for "cross stitch pattern cat" and you'll get hundreds of results. Search for "crochet graph cat" and you'll get far fewer. Cross stitch has been popular for centuries. Designers have charted everything from cartoon characters to holiday designs to portraits. That huge back catalog is free for you to borrow from, as long as you check the license terms on any pattern you didn't chart yourself.

Converting also gives you control. You can resize the image. You can change the stitch count. You can switch colors before you ever pick up a hook or needle. A cross stitch chart is really just a map. You get to decide how to build the road.

## Step 1: Choose the Right Cross Stitch Pattern to Convert

Pick a pattern with clear, blocky shapes and a low stitch count if you're new to converting charts. Complex patterns with fine detail get harder to translate cleanly. Simple shapes convert best.

Cross stitch patterns can have fine detail because embroidery floss makes small, precise X shapes. Crochet stitches and knit stitches are bigger and boxier. A single crochet stitch is roughly square, but it has some texture and it doesn't hold a sharp diagonal line the way thread does.

When picking your pattern, look for:

- **Bold outlines.** Thin one-pixel details often disappear in crochet or knit.
- **Limited colors.** Two to six colors is a good starting range. More colors means more yarn changes and more chances for mistakes.
- **Symmetry, if possible.** Symmetrical designs are easier to check for errors as you go.
- **A grid size under 50 by 50 stitches** for a first project. Bigger charts take longer and are harder to fix if something goes wrong.

If you love a highly detailed pattern, you can still use it. Just know it will take more rows, more color changes, and more patience.

## Step 2: Count the Stitch Grid and Check the Size

Count how many squares wide and how many squares tall the cross stitch chart is. This tells you exactly how many stitches and rows your crochet or knit project will need. Most cross stitch charts include this count in the pattern key.

Look at the top and side of the chart. Most patterns list the grid size, like "80 stitches wide by 100 stitches tall." If it's not listed, count the squares yourself. Zoom in if you're working from a photo or PDF.

Write this number down. It becomes your stitch count and row count for the crochet or knit version. For example, an 80 by 100 chart means:

- 80 stitches per row (or 80 pixels wide, if you're doing C2C)
- 100 rows total

This step also helps you plan yarn amount and finished size, which leads to the next step.

## Step 3: Decide on Your Finished Size Before You Start

Decide how big you want the finished piece to be, then work backward to figure out your stitch gauge. This step happens before you buy yarn or pick a hook size. Skipping it leads to projects that come out much bigger or smaller than expected.

Every square in the chart will become one stitch. So the finished size depends on how big each stitch is. That depends on your yarn weight, hook or needle size, and your own tension.

Here's how to work it out:

1. Decide the finished width you want. Say you want a blanket 40 inches wide.
2. Divide that by your stitch count. If your chart is 80 stitches wide, that's 40 ÷ 80 = 0.5 inches per stitch.
3. Make a small swatch with your planned yarn and hook. Measure 10 stitches across.
4. If those 10 stitches measure 5 inches, you're right at 0.5 inches per stitch. If they measure more or less, adjust your hook size up or down.

Always check your gauge with a real swatch before committing to a big project. A single crochet graphghan can take weeks to make. You don't want to find out at row 50 that it's turning out three feet too wide.

## Step 4: Pick Your Method: Single Crochet, C2C, Tapestry Crochet, or Knit Colorwork

Choose the stitch method based on the look you want and how much color-changing you're willing to do. Single crochet rows and C2C are the two most common choices for cross stitch conversions in crochet. Stranded knitting or intarsia work well if you knit instead.

**Single crochet, row by row (pixel blanket method)**
You work straight rows of single crochet, changing color mid-row as the chart tells you to. This gives a clean, blocky look close to the original cross stitch image. It works well for graphghans and pillow covers. The tradeoff is you carry or cut yarn often, which means more ends to weave in.

**Corner-to-corner (C2C)**
C2C builds the design in diagonal blocks, starting from one corner. Each block acts like one pixel. This method is popular for graphghans because color changes happen at the start of each block, not mid-row, which some crocheters find easier to manage. The finished fabric has a slightly different texture, with visible diagonal ridges.

**Tapestry crochet**
Tapestry crochet carries unused colors along the back of your work instead of cutting them. This works well for patterns with only two or three colors used often throughout, like a simple heart or star pattern. It creates a dense, sturdy fabric, good for bags or coasters.

**Knit colorwork (stranded or intarsia)**
If you knit, you can use the same chart with stranded colorwork (carrying yarn across the back) for small repeating motifs, or intarsia (using separate yarn bobbins per color block) for larger single-use color areas. Knit stitches are usually a bit wider than they are tall, so a square cross stitch chart may come out slightly stretched. Some knitters adjust the chart to account for this by stretching or squashing the image slightly before converting.

Pick the method that matches your project. A small motif on a hat brim might suit stranded knitting. A big graphghan of a favorite cartoon character often suits single crochet rows or C2C.

## Step 5: Convert the Chart, Square by Square

Go through the cross stitch chart one row at a time and match each square to one stitch in your chosen method. Keep a paper or digital copy nearby and mark off each row as you finish it. This keeps you from losing your place.

Here's a simple system:

1. Print or open the chart digitally.
2. Highlight or color in each row as you complete it in yarn.
3. Work left to right for odd rows, right to left for even rows, if you're doing single crochet back and forth (this matches how the stitches stack).
4. For C2C, follow the pattern's diagonal block order instead of straight rows.
5. Double check your color key. Cross stitch charts often use symbols instead of just colors, so confirm which symbol maps to which color before you start.

Take a photo of your progress every few rows. If you make a mistake, this makes it much easier to find where it happened, instead of ripping out your whole project.

## Step 6: Test a Swatch Before Committing to the Full Project

Make a small test swatch using a corner or simple section of the chart before starting the whole thing. This step confirms your gauge, your color choices, and your chosen method all work together the way you pictured.

A swatch might be a 10 by 10 stitch section, maybe a corner of the design or a simple repeating motif. Use it to check:

- Does the color change method you picked work smoothly?
- Are you happy with how blocky or smooth the shapes look?
- Does your gauge match what you calculated in Step 3?
- Is the yarn weight giving you the drape or stiffness you want?

If something feels off, this is the cheap place to fix it. Changing hook size or yarn weight after 10 stitches costs you a few minutes. Changing it after 200 rows costs you hours of ripping out work.

## Step 7: Track Your Yarn Amounts by Color

Count how many squares of each color appear in the chart, then estimate your yarn needs from that count. This step prevents running out of a color halfway through your project, which can be hard to fix if you can't get more from the same dye lot.

To estimate:

1. Count total squares per color in the chart. Many cross stitch pattern software tools and some printed patterns already list this for you as a "floss usage" count.
2. Multiply that number by how much yarn one stitch uses in your gauge. You can find this by weighing your gauge swatch, then dividing the weight by the number of stitches in it.
3. Add about 10 percent extra for tension changes, mistakes, and weaving in ends.

For big blocks of background color, buy a bit more than you think you need. Running short on a small accent color is easier to fix than running short on the main background, especially with hand-dyed yarn where colors vary between batches.

## Step 8: Finish, Block, and Weave In Ends

Weave in all your ends securely, then block your finished piece if the fiber allows it. This final step evens out your stitches and gives the whole design a cleaner, flatter look.

Cross stitch conversions in crochet or knit often have many color changes, which means many yarn ends. Weave each one in along the back, following the path of the stitches so it stays hidden. For single crochet especially, weave ends in multiple directions so they don't pull loose over time.

Blocking helps square up any stitches that came out uneven during color changes. Wet blocking works well for wool and many animal fiber blends. Steam blocking can work for some acrylics, but always check your yarn label first, since some synthetic fibers can melt or flatten under heat.

Once it's blocked and dry, step back and compare it to the original cross stitch chart. You'll likely see your own stitch texture come through, whether that's the diagonal ridges of C2C or the small bumps of single crochet. That's part of what makes a converted chart look different from the original embroidery, and part of what makes it your own version of the design.

## Frequently asked questions

### What is a cross stitch conversion chart used for?
A cross stitch conversion chart translates thread colors between different brands, most commonly DMC to Anchor or Anchor to DMC, so you can substitute floss without losing the pattern's original color scheme. This is essential when a chart calls for a brand you don't have, or when your local craft store only stocks one manufacturer's threads. Conversion charts match colors by shade rather than exact code, since brands don't produce identical dye lots, so the closest equivalent is used to preserve the design's look.

### Are DMC to Anchor conversions always exact matches?
No, DMC to Anchor conversions are approximations rather than perfect matches. Because the two companies use different dyeing processes and color libraries, a conversion chart picks the nearest visual equivalent, which can occasionally look slightly lighter, darker, or warmer than the original. For most projects this difference is unnoticeable once stitched, but for pieces with subtle shading or color gradients, it's worth ordering a sample skein first to confirm the match works for your specific fabric and lighting.

### Can I convert cross stitch thread colors to other brands besides DMC and Anchor?
Yes, many conversion tools also cover brands like Madeira, Sullivan's, JP Coats, and Anchor Marlitt, in addition to the standard DMC and Anchor pairing. A good conversion chart or app lets you search by any brand's code and returns equivalents across multiple manufacturers at once. This is especially useful for vintage patterns or kits from overseas designers, since older or regional charts sometimes reference thread lines that are harder to find today.

### How do I use a conversion chart with an existing cross stitch pattern?
Start by identifying the thread codes listed in your pattern's color key, then look up each one in the conversion chart to find its match in your preferred brand. Replace the codes on your pattern key while keeping the symbol-to-color assignments intact, so you don't lose track of which stitch uses which thread. Many digital tools, including conversion apps, let you input a full color list at once and generate an updated key automatically, saving time on larger patterns.

### Why do DMC and Anchor use different numbering systems?
DMC and Anchor developed their numbering systems independently, each based on their own internal color libraries and manufacturing history rather than a shared industry standard. This means code numbers don't correspond between brands at all, a low DMC number can equal a completely different Anchor shade. That's precisely why conversion charts exist: they cross-reference the actual dyed color rather than the numbering logic, letting stitchers move between brands confidently without guessing.
