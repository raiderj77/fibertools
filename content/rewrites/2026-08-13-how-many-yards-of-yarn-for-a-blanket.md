# How Many Yards of Yarn for a Blanket?

Most blankets take between 1,500 and 4,500 yards of yarn, depending on size and yarn weight. A baby blanket in worsted weight might use 1,200 yards. A queen size afghan in chunky yarn could take 3,500 yards or more. Your stitch pattern, tension, and gauge all change the math.

## Why is there such a big range in yardage?

The range is big because three things change at once: blanket size, yarn weight, and stitch pattern. Each one can swing your total by hundreds of yards.

A lap blanket and a king size bed cover are not close in size. A lap blanket might be 36 by 48 inches. A king size blanket can be 100 by 100 inches or bigger. That is a huge difference in square footage, and yardage scales with it.

Yarn weight matters just as much. Thicker yarn covers more area per yard, but each stitch also uses more length. Thinner yarn needs more stitches to cover the same space, so it partly balances out. Still, a bulky yarn blanket almost always uses fewer total yards than the same size blanket in fingering weight.

Stitch pattern is the wild card. A simple garter stitch or single crochet uses less yarn than a cabled or textured pattern. Cables pull in fabric and need extra length to make the twists. Popcorn stitches, bobbles, and thick textured crochet stitches eat yarn fast. A lacy, open pattern can go the other way and use less yarn than a solid one, because there is less fabric overall.

## How much yarn do I need for a baby blanket?

A baby blanket usually needs 1,000 to 1,800 yards of worsted weight yarn. Size is normally 30 by 36 inches, give or take. Simple stitch patterns land on the lower end, textured ones on the higher end.

Baby blankets are the most forgiving project for yardage guessing, because they are small. Even if your gauge is off, you are not risking a huge yarn shortfall.

Here is a rough breakdown by yarn weight for a 30 by 36 inch blanket:

- **DK weight (light worsted):** 1,400 to 1,800 yards
- **Worsted weight:** 1,000 to 1,400 yards
- **Chunky weight:** 700 to 1,000 yards

These numbers assume a plain stitch like stockinette, garter, or single crochet. Add 10 to 20 percent more yarn if you are using cables, shells, or a dense crochet stitch like the moss stitch or the crocodile stitch.

## How much yarn do I need for a throw or lap blanket?

A throw blanket, around 50 by 60 inches, typically takes 2,000 to 3,000 yards of worsted weight yarn. This is the most common blanket size people knit or crochet as gifts. Bulkier yarn can bring that number down closer to 1,500 yards.

Throws are popular because they finish faster than a full bed blanket but still feel substantial. Here is what to expect by yarn weight for a standard 50 by 60 inch throw:

- **Worsted weight:** 2,000 to 3,000 yards
- **Chunky weight:** 1,200 to 1,800 yards
- **Super bulky weight:** 700 to 1,200 yards
- **DK weight:** 2,800 to 3,800 yards

If you are crocheting instead of knitting, expect to use a bit more yarn for the same size. Crochet stitches, especially double crochet and taller stitches, tend to use more yarn per square inch than knit stitches like stockinette.

## How much yarn do I need for a full, queen, or king size blanket?

A full or queen size bed blanket, roughly 90 by 90 inches, usually takes 3,500 to 5,000 yards of worsted weight yarn. A king size blanket can run 4,500 to 6,000 yards or more. These are big projects, so small changes in gauge add up to a lot of yarn.

At this size, you are covering serious square footage. A queen size blanket has about 8,100 square inches of surface area. Compare that to a throw at 3,000 square inches. You are looking at almost triple the fabric, so triple the yarn is a fair starting estimate.

Rough numbers by yarn weight for a 90 by 90 inch blanket:

- **Worsted weight:** 3,500 to 5,000 yards
- **Chunky weight:** 2,200 to 3,200 yards
- **DK weight:** 5,000 to 6,500 yards

For a king size blanket at 108 by 108 inches or larger, add another 20 to 30 percent to these numbers.

Big blankets are where it pays to buy from the same dye lot. Running out partway through a queen size blanket and having to match colors later is a real headache.

## Does my stitch pattern really change the yardage that much?

Yes, stitch pattern can change your yardage by 20 to 50 percent compared to a plain stitch. Cables, bobbles, and dense crochet stitches use noticeably more yarn than stockinette, garter stitch, or single crochet.

Think about what a cable actually does. It crosses stitches over each other, which pulls the fabric in and makes it shorter and narrower for the same number of stitches. To get the same finished size as a plain piece, you need more stitches and more rows, which means more yarn.

Bobbles and popcorn stitches work the same way. Each bobble is made from several stitches worked into one spot, then gathered back down. That extra fabric has to go somewhere, and it makes the yarn use go up.

On the crochet side, taller stitches use more yarn per stitch, but you need fewer of them to cover the same area. A double crochet blanket and a single crochet blanket of the same size often end up close in total yardage, but double crochet works up faster.

Openwork patterns, like lace knitting or crochet mesh stitches, use less yarn because there is less actual fabric. A lacy shawl-style blanket might use half the yarn of a solid one at the same dimensions.

If you are following a pattern, check the yardage the designer lists. If you are designing your own blanket or swapping stitch patterns, knit or crochet a swatch first. Weigh it, then calculate how much yarn per square inch that swatch used. Multiply that by your total blanket area to get a real estimate.

## How do I calculate yardage for my own blanket size?

The best way is to knit or crochet a gauge swatch, weigh it, and scale up the numbers to your full blanket size. This method works no matter what stitch, yarn, or size you are using.

Here is the process, step by step:

1. **Make a swatch.** At least 4 by 4 inches, in your actual stitch pattern and yarn.
2. **Weigh the swatch** on a kitchen scale, in grams.
3. **Check the yarn label** for total yards and total grams in a full skein.
4. **Calculate yards used per gram.** Divide the yarn's total yardage by its total weight in grams. This gives you yards per gram for that specific yarn.
5. **Calculate your swatch's yardage.** Multiply your swatch weight by the yards per gram number from step 4.
6. **Find your swatch's area.** Multiply the swatch width by the swatch height in inches.
7. **Get yards per square inch.** Divide swatch yardage by swatch area.
8. **Calculate your total blanket area.** Multiply your planned blanket width by height in inches.
9. **Multiply total area by yards per square inch.** This gives you your estimated total yardage.

Here is an example. Say your swatch is 4 by 4 inches, which is 16 square inches. It weighs 10 grams. Your yarn label says 200 yards per 100 grams, so that is 2 yards per gram. Your swatch used 20 yards (10 grams times 2 yards per gram). Divide 20 yards by 16 square inches, and you get 1.25 yards per square inch.

If your blanket is 50 by 60 inches, that is 3,000 square inches. Multiply 3,000 by 1.25, and you get 3,750 yards needed.

This method beats guessing from a chart because it uses your actual gauge, your actual yarn, and your actual stitch pattern. Always check your gauge before you commit to buying yarn for a big project.

## Should I buy extra yarn just in case?

Yes, buy at least 10 percent more yarn than your estimate calls for. Blankets are large projects, and running out one skein short near the end is common and frustrating.

Dye lots matter here too. Yarn dye lots can vary slightly in color, even within the same colorway. If you run out and have to buy more later, there is a real chance the new skein will not match perfectly, especially with hand-dyed or semi-solid yarns.

Buying extra also protects you against gauge drift. Many knitters and crocheters tighten up or loosen their tension over a long project, especially one worked over weeks or months. That drift can change your yardage needs from your original swatch calculation.

If you are working from a pattern, buy the yardage the pattern recommends plus a bit extra, rather than trying to shave costs by buying the exact number listed. Patterns usually already include a small buffer, but it is not always generous.

For scrap or stash blankets, like granny square throws or knit strip blankets, this math matters less. You can plan those projects around whatever yarn you already have, and adjust the final size to match your supply.

## What is the fastest way to estimate yardage without doing math?

Check the yardage listed on patterns for a similar size and stitch pattern to yours. Many knitting and crochet pattern databases list finished yardage alongside the finished measurements, which gives you a real-world number to work from.

The Craft Yarn Council also publishes standard body and blanket measurements, which can help you figure out what size category your blanket falls into. Matching your planned blanket to a similar existing pattern is often faster than doing the swatch math yourself, especially for a first blanket project.

Just remember that yardage listed on a pattern is based on the designer's gauge and yarn choice. If you substitute a different yarn or change your hook or needle size, your actual usage can shift. When in doubt, swatch first and measure as you go.

## Frequently asked questions

### How many yards of yarn do I need for a throw blanket?
A throw blanket typically requires 1,200 to 1,800 yards of worsted weight yarn. This range covers standard sizes around 50x60 inches, but the exact amount depends on stitch pattern density, hook or needle size, and tension. Cable stitches and dense crochet patterns use more yarn than simple garter or single crochet. If you're using a bulkier yarn, you'll need fewer yards but more weight in ounces. Always buy an extra skein or two to avoid dye lot mismatches, especially with hand-dyed or variegated yarns where running short mid-project is frustrating.

### How much yarn do I need for a queen size blanket?
A queen size blanket needs approximately 4,500 to 6,000 yards of worsted weight yarn, depending on stitch choice. Queen blankets typically measure 90x90 inches or larger, making them one of the biggest yarn investments in fiber crafts. Lacy or open stitches use less yarn per square inch, while dense crochet or cabled knitting can push you toward the higher end of that range. Using a yarn requirement calculator based on your specific gauge swatch gives more accurate results than general estimates, since individual tension varies significantly between crafters.

### Does crochet use more yarn than knitting for a blanket?
Yes, crochet generally uses 20-30% more yarn than knitting for the same size blanket. This happens because crochet stitches create thicker fabric with more yarn wrapped per stitch, especially with double or treble crochet. Knitting produces a flatter, more yarn-efficient fabric, particularly with simple stitches like stockinette. If you're substituting a knitting pattern for crochet or vice versa, always add a buffer of 15-25% extra yardage to your estimate. Testing a gauge swatch in your actual pattern stitch is the most reliable way to predict real yarn consumption.

### How do I calculate yarn yardage for a custom blanket size?
Calculate custom blanket yardage by multiplying your blanket's square footage by the yarn usage rate of your gauge swatch. First, knit or crochet a 4x4 inch swatch in your chosen stitch and yarn weight, then measure exactly how many yards it used. Divide that by 16 to get yards per square inch, then multiply by your total blanket area. This method accounts for your personal tension and specific stitch pattern, giving far more accurate results than generic size charts, which assume average gauge and standard yarn weights.

### What yarn weight uses the least yardage for a blanket?
Bulky and super bulky yarns use the least total yardage because each stitch covers more surface area. A super bulky throw blanket might only need 600-900 yards compared to 1,200-1,800 yards of worsted weight for the same size. However, bulky yarn blankets require more total fiber by weight and cost, since thicker yarn is heavier per yard. This makes bulky yarn ideal for quick projects when you want fewer yards to track and faster progress, though it typically increases your overall yarn budget compared to thinner weights.
